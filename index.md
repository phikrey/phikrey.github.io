How does our brain enable us to perceive and interact with the world around us? 
This question is the central focus of my research. During my PhD, I used eye movements and psychophysics to understand how our brain uses visual information to construct our perception and to guide our actions. Eye movements are an essential part of our visual experience. We constantly move our eyes to gather the most relevant visual information for a task at hand. Eye movements thus closely reflect how we sample visual information that we use interact with objects in our environment. One part of my research aims to translate the fundamental knowledge gained from studying healthy control subjects to clinical populations to understand how eye movements can be used to understand perceptual impairments in patients with chronic brain damage to their visual system.

During my postdoc, I extend this line of research and ask: How does the brain integrate our internal state with external sensory information? Our excitability, engagement, and arousal constantly fluctuate throughout the day. These state changes can massive, widespread effects on our brain. Yet, how these changes in our brain state affect how we process visual information is poorly understood. To study this question, I combine functional neuroimaging with psychophysics, eye tracking, and physiological measurements.

I am currently a postdoctoral fellow working with Dr. Elisha P. Merriam in the Computational Neuroimaging and Perception Team in Bethesda, MD. I studied Psychology in Munich, Germany and Glasgow, UK and completed my PhD at the University of British Columbia, Canada under the mentorship of Dr. Miriam Spering.
