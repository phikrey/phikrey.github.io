Visual processing for the control of short-latency, reflexive eye movements

Motor circuits in the brain have privileged access to visual information, allowing us to react to incoming sensory signals with minimal delays. Eye movements, such as ocular following or express saccades, occur at ultra-short latencies (80-100 ms) and allow us to study the mechanisms of fast visuomotor reflexes. Some of this work was conducted in collaboration with Drs. J. Anthony Movshon (New York University, USA) and Douglas Althuler (University of British Columbia, Canada). I am currently extending this line of research to investigate how chronic brain damage to primary visual cortex affects reflexive eye movements in collaboration with Dr. Krystel Huxlin (University of Rochester, USA).

Coordination of visual guided eye and hand movements

Interacting with moving objects is a fundamental ability that underlies many tasks of daily living, from catching a ball to crossing a street. Due to inherent sensorimotor delays, accurate interaction with moving objects requires the ability to the future position of an object in order to accurately hit, catch, or otherwise intercept it. Eye movements play a critical role in forming visual predictions. During my undergraduate and graduate studies at the Ludwig Maximilian University (Munich, Germany) and the University of British Columbia (Vancouver, Canada), I studied how eye and hand movements are coordinated during predictive actions.

Effects of brain state on visual processing and visual perception

Our brain undergoes massive changes in its internal dynamics and responsiveness to external sensory stimuli as we switch from a state of drowsiness (while driving for hours on an empty highway) to a state of heightened alertness (while merging into fast-moving rush hour traffic). Although primary visual cortex has traditionally been viewed as a purely sensory brain area, recent work has shown that engagement, arousal, and spontaneous body movements can massively influence brain activity in V1. My current postdoctoral work tries to understand how these non-visual, arousal-related signals interact with visual processing and affect visual perception. To study these questions in humans, I combine eye-tracking, physiological recordings (pulse and breathing rate) with ultra high-field fMRI at 7T.
